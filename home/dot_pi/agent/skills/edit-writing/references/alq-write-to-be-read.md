# Write to be read

**When you send an email to announce something, you assume you will be read. However chances are, you won’t.**

Every week I receive a number of engineering updates in my inbox via [eng-projects@datadoghq.com](mailto:eng-projects@datadoghq.com). This practice is great:

1. It’s a opportunity for teams to share all the cool stuff they’ve been working on.
2. It encourages clarity in the mind of the author by asking the questions of what matters in the recent deliveries or problems.
3. It lets the reader know about an update on something they may depend on. And that update may alter their own plans.

**But that only works if and only if these updates are actually read**. And I doubt that most are. I know I struggle to read through a lot of them. That is a problem, because the work you’ve put into putting them together goes to waste.

# Why aren’t your updates read?

The gist of the argument is this: **the only goal of your announcement email is to change the reader’s view of the world.** If you have not changed the reader’s mind, then you’ve wasted your time and theirs.

To be sure, you write to help you think, but that’s not what you should send. Writing to think belongs to your notebook, your drafts, your outlines, your journal, etc.

# What do you need to change?

I’ve tried to make a simple list.

1. **Use simple words**. No “streamline”, no “strategic”, no “synergy”. Jargon obfuscates.
2. **Be precise**. Find the simplest word that describes what you mean with the right level of detail. “I need more resources” could mean anything: “people”, “money”, “materials”, “access”.
3. **Boil it down to the essential**. In the case of engineering updates, you can try to limit yourself to:
   1. Our most notable success of the period, and why it is the most notable.
   2. Our most notable challenge or failure, and why.
   3. Our most notable question or blocker, and why.
4. **Most important point first a/k/a Don’t bury the lede**. Like you, the reader has other things to do so don’t waste the first paragraph with a long description of who you are, what your group does, etc. Make it a one-line sentence and move on.
5. **If you truly need to share more details, make sure you have the reader’s attention.** Put them below the fold. Maybe you allow yourself a second-most important things we did/failed at/are blocked by.

I hope you’re still with me.

## Footer comments (from Confluence)

> A great example of writing to be read is beginning organization-wide “notice” emails with:
>
> > _(if your team does not use \<internal service / tool name>, you can stop reading this message)._
>
> There’s inevitably going to be lots of ongoing migrations and changes across the organization that need to be communicated broadly. Not every service or team has a dedicated email list for its users, so these notices are often shared broadly.
> When I see this line at the beginning of an email, it (a) saves me time as a reader, (b) gives a hint at *why* the change matters to me, and (c) shows the author’s respect for others time.
